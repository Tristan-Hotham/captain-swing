On the night of {{context.first_machine_date|raw}}, labourers at {{context.first_machine_place|raw}} broke a threshing machine. By December the trouble had spread across the south and east of England: machines smashed, ricks and barns set on fire, overseers of the poor carted out of their parishes, and threatening letters signed by a man who never existed, "Captain Swing". It was the largest rural uprising in nineteenth-century England, and the first great revolt of workers against a labour-saving machine.

Historians have argued about it ever since. Was it about the threshing machine, which took away the winter work of beating corn with a flail? Or was it about hunger, low wages, the enclosure of common land and a harsh poor law, with the machine as just the most visible target? In 2020 the economists Bruno Caprettini and Hans-Joachim Voth mapped every Swing incident and every threshing machine they could find onto the {{counts.parishes_all|int}} parishes of 1851 England and Wales, and published all of it openly. I have used their data to draw the riots, the machines and the rival suspects side by side, and to ask the plain question: did the riots follow the machines?

My short answer: yes, but not only. Parishes with a recorded machine were about twice as likely to riot, even compared with their own neighbours. Yet nine in ten riotous parishes had no recorded machine, enclosure explains almost nothing, and the parishes that spent most on poor relief were among the most rebellious. The machine was a spark, and the countryside was already dry.

01 · The run-upMachines arrive, then fires

Caprettini and Voth found threshing machines in two kinds of source: {{counts.machine_records_ads|int}} advertisements in provincial newspapers, mostly farm sales listing a machine among the stock, and {{counts.machine_records_gva|int}} machines described in the county General View of Agriculture surveys. Together they place {{counts.machines_all|int}} machines in {{counts.machine_parishes_all|int}} parishes before 1830. They also combed the newspapers for earlier unrest, finding {{counts.pre1830_episodes|int}} reports of rural arson and machine-breaking from {{counts.pre1830_first_year|raw}} to {{counts.pre1830_last_year|raw}}.

The fire map is unmistakable. Swing was above all a southern and eastern rising: Kent, Sussex, Hampshire, Berkshire and Wiltshire burn brightest, with a second front in Norfolk and Suffolk. Yet the single parish with most incidents in the data is {{top_parishes.0.parish|raw}} in {{top_parishes.0.county|raw}}, with {{top_parishes.0.incidents|int}}, followed by {{top_parishes.1.parish|raw}} ({{top_parishes.1.incidents|int}}) and {{top_parishes.2.parish|raw}} ({{top_parishes.2.incidents|int}}). Five counties, Kent, Norfolk, Wiltshire, Hampshire and Sussex, hold {{counties.top5_incident_share|pct0}} of all incidents in the main sample. Swing also reached further than its reputation: the North-West region, which includes Shropshire, Cheshire and Lancashire, still saw unrest in {{regions.1.riot_share|pct0}} of its parishes, against {{regions.0.riot_share|pct0}} in the South-East and {{regions.4.riot_share|pct1}} in Wales.

The machine map is patchier, and that is a warning: these are the machines that happened to be advertised or described, not every machine that existed.

Kent shows the problem best. Swing began there with a wave of machine-breaking, yet the data record only {{counties.top.0.machines|int}} machines in its {{counties.top.0.parishes|int}} parishes. Whatever the reason, perhaps that Kent's machines rarely appeared in the papers that were searched, the recorded machines are a thin sample. Any parish-level comparison of "machine" and "no machine" parishes therefore compares parishes with a recorded machine against a mix of parishes with none and parishes whose machine left no trace.

03 · The machinesDid the riots follow the machines?

Start with the simplest comparison. Of the {{machines.n_with|int}} parishes in Caprettini and Voth's main sample with at least one recorded machine, {{machines.riot_share_with|pct1}} saw some Swing unrest. Of the {{machines.n_without|int}} without one, {{machines.riot_share_without|pct1}} did. That is {{machines.ratio|f1}} times the rate. Machine-breaking itself was {{machines.attack_ratio|f1}} times as common in machine parishes ({{machines.attack_share_with|pct1}} against {{machines.attack_share_without|pct1}}), which you would expect, but so were fires, letters and wage riots ({{machines.other_share_with|pct1}} against {{machines.other_share_without|pct1}}).

The obvious objection is geography. Machines were commonest in the corn-growing south and east, which is also where Swing was. So I compared within regions and, more strictly, with each machine parish's own neighbours. In {{machines.regions_higher_with|words}} of the five regions, machine parishes rioted more often (Wales, with {{regions.4.n_with|int}} machine parishes and almost no unrest, is the exception). Against the machine-free parishes within {{neighbours.radius_km|int}} km, on average {{neighbours.mean_neighbours|f1}} of them, machine parishes were {{neighbours.diff|pp1}} percentage points more likely to riot ({{neighbours.machine_riot_share|pct1}} against {{neighbours.neighbour_riot_share|pct1}}; 95% interval {{neighbours.ci_lo|pp1}} to {{neighbours.ci_hi|pp1}} points). A simple regression that also holds distance from London, population density, the farm share of workers and region constant gives a similar gap of {{models.0.terms.machine.b|pp1}} points (standard error {{models.0.terms.machine.se|pp1}}).

So the riots did follow the machines. But look at it the other way round. Of the {{counts.riot_parishes_sample|int}} parishes in the main sample with any Swing unrest, {{machines.riot_parishes_without_machine_share|pct0}} had no recorded machine. And {{machines.machine_parishes_quiet_share|pct0}} of machine parishes stayed quiet. Machine-breaking was only {{counts.attack_share_of_incidents|pct0}} of all incidents; most were arson, threatening letters, wage riots and attacks on overseers and parsons.

Their test, and why it is clever

A raw gap can mislead: perhaps the farmers who bought machines were also the hardest employers. Caprettini and Voth use the soil. Threshing machines of the time worked badly on heavy clay land, which grew less wheat, so parishes on heavy soil adopted fewer machines for reasons that had nothing to do with local politics. In their data, a parish wholly on heavy soil had {{repro.fs_heavy|abs3}} fewer recorded machines and {{repro.rf_heavy|abs3}} fewer Swing incidents than one with none, other things equal. Their instrumental-variable estimate, which I reproduce exactly from their files, is that each extra machine caused about {{repro.iv7_thresh|f1}} more incidents (standard error {{repro.iv7_se|f1}}). That is their causal result, not mine; the size of the number partly reflects that the recorded machines stand for many unrecorded ones.

04 · The rival suspectsHunger, wages, enclosure and the poor law

If the machines were only part of the story, what else lines up with the riots? The data hold several of the classic suspects, measured in different ways and for different sets of parishes. The chart lines them up: for each, the parishes are split into five equal groups (four for unemployment) from lowest to highest, and the dots show the share of each group that saw Swing unrest. A steep line means a strong association; a flat one means none.

All measures from Caprettini and Voth's data: enclosure from Gonner (1912); poor rates per head in 1801 and winter unemployment in 1834 from the Poor Law Report of 1834; wheat prices at the nearest market town, 1821 to 1830, from Brunt and Cannon's corn returns; farm share of workers from the 1801 census; heavy soil from the British Geological Survey.

Enclosure is the great surprise, or rather the great non-event. Across the five groups, from parishes in districts with no recorded enclosure of commons by 1820 to the most enclosed, the share with unrest moves only from {{gradients.enclosures.rows.0.riot_share|pct1}} to {{gradients.enclosures.rows.4.riot_share|pct1}}. This fits the old observation by the historian G. E. Mingay that the heavily enclosed Midlands stayed quiet in 1830. The measure is coarse (a district estimate scaled back to 1820), but there is no sign of a strong link.

The poor law is different. Among the {{gradients.poor.n|int}} parishes with figures, those that spent the least per head on poor relief in 1801 had unrest in {{gradients.poor.rows.0.riot_share|pct0}} of cases; those that spent the most, {{gradients.poor.rows.4.riot_share|pct0}}. In the south, poor relief often topped up low wages, so high rates mark places where farm wages were too low to live on. Winter unemployment, reported to the Poor Law inquiry in 1834, shows a similar rise, from {{gradients.unemp.rows.0.riot_share|pct0}} to {{gradients.unemp.rows.3.riot_share|pct0}}, in the {{gradients.unemp.n|int}} parishes that answered. Winter was exactly when hand threshing used to provide work. But this was measured after the riots, by parishes that chose to reply, so treat it as a clue, not a test.

Hunger, at least as measured by the price of wheat at the nearest market in the 1820s, shows no clear pattern (from {{gradients.wheat.rows.0.riot_share|pct0}} to {{gradients.wheat.rows.4.riot_share|pct0}}). The data hold no parish wages, so I cannot test wages directly; poor rates are the nearest proxy. Parishes where more of the workforce was on the land were, if anything, a little less likely to riot, and those on heavy clay, where machines were rarer, were less likely too.

Put the suspects into one model and the machine gap holds in the full sample and the enclosure sample, while poor rates and winter unemployment carry their own weight in their smaller samples, where the machine gap is no longer precisely measured. The honest reading is that the machine mattered, and so did poverty, and the data cannot cleanly separate them outside the parishes where both are recorded.

05 · The spreadFrom Elham outwards, and London's ring

The data carry no dates for individual Swing incidents, so I cannot animate the autumn of 1830 week by week. What they do carry is distance. Caprettini and Voth measure every parish's distance from Elham in East Kent, near where the first machines were broken, to capture how the movement spread from its starting point. 06 · An asideRobots took our jobs? What 1830 can and cannot tell us

A light, clearly labelled detour. Everything here is about 1830; I make no claims about any modern technology's effects.

The threshing machine is a lovely test case for a question we keep asking about labour-saving technology, from looms to software. Hand threshing with a flail was the great winter job of the farm year; by the eighteenth century it took {{context.threshing_labour_share|raw}} of agricultural labour, according to Wikipedia's summary of the history. A horse-driven machine did the same work far faster. Using the {{productivity.by_power.horses.n|int}} horse-powered machines with worker and output figures in the General Views, and Caprettini and Voth's benchmark of {{productivity.flail|f1}} bushels per man-hour with a flail, a horse machine threshed about {{productivity.by_power.horses.mean|f1}} bushels per worker-hour on average, roughly {{productivity.horses_ratio|f0}} times the flail.

Bushels threshed per worker-hour, counting women and children as half a man, as in Caprettini and Voth's appendix. Flail benchmark: their figure, citing Gregory Clark. Few machines, rough figures: read the order of magnitude, not the decimals.

So what does the first great "robots took our jobs" revolt tell us? Three things the data can support, and three it cannot.

It can tell us that people did respond to the machine where it arrived: parishes with a recorded machine rioted about twice as often, even against their neighbours. It can tell us that the response was sharpest where there was nowhere else to go. Far from the industrial towns (beyond about {{outside.median_km|f0}} km), machine parishes rioted {{outside.distant.gap|pp1}} points more often than machine-free ones; close to them, where a labourer might find other work, the gap was {{outside.close.gap|pp1}} points. Caprettini and Voth make the same point with their own models. And it can tell us that the machine landed on a population already squeezed: the riots tracked poverty and poor relief as well as machines, and most riotous parishes had no recorded machine at all.

Share of parishes with any Swing unrest, split at Caprettini and Voth's median distance to the nearest of 15 manufacturing towns.

It cannot tell us what any modern technology will do: a threshing machine is not software, and an 1830 parish with no welfare state, no vote for labourers and no other employer for miles is not a modern labour market. It cannot tell us whether the machine made rural England poorer or richer in the long run; these data stop in 1832. And it cannot tell us whether the anger was about the machine itself or about who captured its gains; the rioters' own letters asked for higher wages as well as an end to machines.

The fair lesson is modest. A labour-saving machine arriving in a place with few other jobs and a frayed safety net was associated with a sharp jump in unrest. The machine alone did not light the fire.

08 · MethodsHow I did this, and what to be careful of

Data. I read Caprettini and Voth's parish cross-section ({{counts.parishes_all|int}} parishes of 1851), their decade panel, and their raw files of dated machine adverts, survey machines and pre-1830 unrest, all in place from their openICPSR package. I computed each parish's centroid from their 1851 boundary file and simplified the outlines for the web. Unless stated, comparisons use their main sample of {{counts.parishes_sample|int}} parishes, the set used in their tables, which leaves out parishes without complete 1801 census measures.

Swing incidents. Their riot counts come from Holland's Swing Unmasked (2005), matched to parishes by Caprettini and Voth; a small number of incidents could not be placed. An "incident" can be a fire, a broken machine, a threatening letter or a riot; counts are not people. "Any unrest" means at least one incident between August 1830 and 1832.

Machines. The machines are those advertised in the newspapers searched or described in the agricultural surveys. Many machines left no such trace (Kent is the clearest case), so "no recorded machine" does not mean "no machine". Errors of this kind tend to shrink simple comparisons towards zero, and an instrument such as their soil test is one way round them.

Comparisons. Neighbour comparisons use, for each machine parish, the share with unrest among machine-free sample parishes whose inside point lies within {{neighbours.radius_km|int}} km, with a bootstrap interval over machine parishes ({{neighbours.n_pairs|int}} of them). Quintile gradients split parishes into equal-sized groups by rank. The linear probability models are descriptive, with robust standard errors; they do not deal with the selection of parishes into the Poor Law returns, with reverse causation for the 1834 unemployment figures, or with unrecorded machines.

Dates. The Swing incidents in this package carry no dates, so the map cannot show the weekly spread of autumn 1830; the play button animates the dated machine adverts and pre-1830 reports instead. Survey machines are dated only to the decade.
